# Content Craft

Use this guidance when creating or revising a written artifact. The brief governs: respect its purpose, audience, format, voice, length, source material, destination, and acceptance criteria. Writing can be technical or nontechnical. Do not turn every task into repository documentation or a software-engineering exercise.

## Start with the reader and purpose

Identify what the reader knows, needs, feels, or should be able to do. Choose detail and terminology accordingly; explain necessary terms without condescension. Keep the central purpose visible, and make every section earn its place. Use a voice consistent with the assignment and maintain it across the piece. Prefer concrete, specific language to stock phrasing, filler, repeated conclusions, and unsupported superlatives. Vary sentence length for pacing; use sensory detail and scenes when they serve narrative, not as decoration in every format.

Explain abstract ideas through examples familiar to this audience. Analogies are aids, not proofs: identify the useful correspondence and where it breaks down. Avoid making a technically false claim just to preserve a memorable comparison. Distinguish illustrative code or scenarios from examples actually executed or observed.

## Shape the piece to its form

Use only as much structure as the material needs. An article or blog post benefits from a clear lead, coherent progression, useful headings when length warrants them, and a conclusion that advances rather than repeats the opening. A tutorial should identify prerequisites, guide actions in a dependable order, describe expected results, and anticipate consequential errors. A story should make character desire, stakes, causality, and change legible while respecting its chosen genre and point of view. Podcast and video scripts should read naturally aloud, distinguish spoken words from production notes, and account for pacing, transitions, and the listener or viewer's context. A screenplay should make scene headings, action, and dialogue clear and playable without explaining what an audience can see. A documentary script should make the relationship between sourced fact, narration, and interpretation clear. These are format-sensitive considerations, not mandatory outlines; follow the requested form and scale.

### Format details when useful

- **Articles and blogs:** choose a specific title, a lead that establishes relevance, and descriptive headings if they aid navigation. Develop a coherent argument with supported examples. An outline or summary can help a long piece, but fixed section counts, mandatory hooks, and universal TL;DRs are not requirements. Use Markdown or the publication's supplied conventions.
- **Tutorials:** state supported versions only when evidenced, prerequisites, ordered steps, expected outcomes, and meaningful troubleshooting. Keep code fences language-tagged and commands copyable where appropriate. Never describe a snippet as tested unless it was actually executed; writer cannot run shell-based checks.
- **Stories:** sustain point of view, distinguish character voices through motivation and dialogue, and make cause and effect legible. Balance scene, summary, and sensory detail with the desired pace. Conventional arcs are options, not mandates; respect an intentional fragment, ambiguity, or unconventional structure.
- **Podcasts:** label speakers consistently, separate sound/music and delivery cues from spoken words, and write dialogue that sounds natural rather than like alternating essays. Read it mentally aloud to revise rhythm. Runtime and timing notes are estimates unless measured; account for pauses, music, and multiple speakers.
- **Videos:** distinguish narration/dialogue from on-screen text, visual direction, and production notes. Mark chapters or timestamps if requested; do not invent footage availability, production feasibility, or measured timing.
- **Screenplays:** use the requested house style or a readable text convention: `INT./EXT. LOCATION - TIME`, present-tense visible action, clear character labels, and dialogue. Use parentheticals sparingly. Text formatting alone is not proof of industry-standard pagination or production readiness.
- **Documentaries:** label narration, interviews, and visual notes distinctly. Tie factual assertions to supplied evidence. Do not invent interview quotes or imply access to footage; label proposed interview questions and suggested visuals as proposals.

## Ground factual claims

Ground source-dependent factual claims in evidence actually supplied or read using permitted tools. Writer children can inspect local materials only; parent sessions may also use their permitted research tools. Do not invent citations, quotations, statistics, names, credentials, research, or source checks. Do not cite a URL whose contents were not provided or read. Keep attribution attached to the claim it supports, distinguish fact from interpretation, and qualify uncertainty when evidence is incomplete. If a factual requirement cannot be met from available material, ask the parent through the report by identifying the missing evidence; do not fill the gap with confident-sounding invention.

Fiction may invent people, events, and dialogue within the requested premise. Keep that invention inside the fictional frame; never disguise invented real-world sourcing, quotations, or endorsements as fact. For creative nonfiction, preserve the distinction between remembered or documented material and dramatization when the brief requires it.

## Revise with intent

When editing, preserve meaning, factual nuance, and the author's intended voice unless the brief authorizes changing them. Improve clarity, sequence, rhythm, and precision without adding unsupported detail. Check consistency of names, terms, tense, point of view, and formatting. Read mentally aloud when sound or dialogue matters. Check the finished work against the actual brief rather than a generic checklist, and report only checks truly performed.

## Scope and limits

This skill grants no tools or permissions. In the parent, ask focused questions when essential requirements are missing and research through available permitted tools when accuracy requires it; avoid over-asking when the brief is sufficient. Writer children cannot ask the user or access the web: report missing essential information to the parent rather than pretending to research.

Use only the tools and files explicitly available to the assigned role. Treat assignment boundaries as workflow constraints, not as a claim that tools enforce filesystem confinement. Do not create or overwrite a file when the task lacks a clear safe destination. Preserve unrelated work and do not publish. Save the complete content in assigned artifacts; return concise artifact paths, sources actually used, checks performed, and unresolved issues in the required report rather than copying long-form prose into bounded report fields. No craft guidance guarantees accuracy beyond examined evidence, publication readiness, reception, or publication outcomes.
